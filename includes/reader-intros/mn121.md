Ānanda reminds the Buddha that he once said, "These days I mostly dwell in
*emptiness*," and asks whether he heard it right. The Buddha says he did,
and explains what he means.

He starts with the room they are sitting in. This mansion, he says, is empty
of elephants and cattle, of gold and silver, of crowds of men and women.
What is not empty is the community of monks. Emptiness here is always empty
of something, and something always remains.

Then he lays out a gradual practice built on that pattern. Leave aside the
village and the people, and attend only to the forest. What has dropped away
is no longer disturbing you; what remains is a smaller disturbance. Then
leave aside the forest for the earth, the earth for boundless space, and so
on through ever subtler states, each time noticing what is now absent and
what is still present.

At the last step even the subtlest state is seen as something put together
and intended, and so impermanent. Seeing that, the heart is released, and
all that remains is this living body with its senses.

It pairs naturally with MN 122, the longer discourse on emptiness.
