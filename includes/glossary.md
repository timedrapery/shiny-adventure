*[Tathāgata]: A title for the Buddha, traditionally left untranslated. It means roughly "the one who has arrived at things as they truly are."

*[Blessed One]: One of the Buddha's traditional titles, used respectfully in place of his name in these texts.

*[spiritual life]: The Buddhist path of practice as a whole -- not just a set of beliefs, but a whole way of living.

*[undeclared]: A question the Buddha deliberately left unanswered -- not because he didn't know, but because answering it wouldn't help anyone actually practice.

*[world]: In this sutta, "the world" is being asked about as a metaphysical whole -- is it eternal, is it finite -- not used in the ordinary everyday sense elsewhere on this page.

*[soul]: This translation's rendering of "jīva" -- the felt sense of an inner self or life-essence. Whether it exists, and whether it's the same as the body, is one of the questions this sutta leaves undeclared.

*[body]: Here specifically the physical body, as it's paired with "soul" in one of the undeclared questions.

*[arrow]: The sutta's central image: a person struck by an arrow thickly smeared with poison who refuses treatment until every background fact about the arrow and the archer is known first -- a warning against blocking practice with unanswerable questions.

*[nibbāna]: The end of greed, hatred, and confusion -- the traditional goal of Buddhist practice. Left untranslated because no single English word covers it; "quenching" is the closest one-word gloss.

*[disenchantment]: A cooling of fascination with ordinary experience -- not depression, but the natural next step once you see clearly how things actually work.

*[fading]: The fading away of attraction and investment in experience, as passion cools.

*[cessation]: The ending of dissatisfaction and its causes -- what practice is ultimately for.

*[calming]: A settling and quieting of the mind on the way toward full release.

*[higher knowing]: Direct, personally realized understanding -- not a special power, just knowing something for yourself rather than taking it on faith.

*[awakening]: Coming fully awake to how things are. Sometimes translated "enlightenment," a word this project avoids as needlessly mystical.

*[dissatisfaction]: This translation's rendering of "dukkha," the Buddhist term for the unsatisfying, unstable quality of ordinary experience. Often translated "suffering," but that can sound more dramatic than the everyday sense of things not quite being right.
