# AN 6.63 Translation Notes

This document is the companion rationale for
[AN 6.63: Nibbedhika Sutta](an6-63-nibbedhika-sutta.md). The main translation
is the primary study surface; this file records debated translation choices,
source-audit calls, and edition notes that govern it.

## Source Base

- Primary source: the Pali text of AN 6.63 as segmented in SuttaCentral's
  Bilara data (142 segments).
- Control surface: the repository's current term policy, especially `kamma`,
  `cetanā`, `vipāka`, `kāma`, `vedanā`, `saññā`, `āsava`, `dukkha`, `nirodha`,
  `nidāna`, `paṭipadā`, and the eight path factors, together with the governed
  four-truths wording in SN 56.11 and the governed distress line shared by
  MN 148 and SN 36.6.
- Working method: the discourse was governed as one repeated analytical frame
  applied six times, rather than as six separate teachings. Every topic is
  taken through the same six aspects in the same wording, because that
  sameness is the point of the exposition.

## Why This Surface Was Needed

`kamma` is a policy-bearing major headword, and before this surface its only
citation anchor was this untranslated discourse. For an early-Buddhist lexicon
that was the most conspicuous gap the Wave 6 audit found.

The line that matters most is `cetanāhaṁ, bhikkhave, kammaṁ vadāmi` — intention
is what I call action. That definition now governs running text.

Note a correction the audit itself needed. The roadmap claimed this discourse
also anchors `phala` and `papa`. It does not: AN 6.63 uses `vipāka` throughout
and never `phala`, and contains no `pāp` at all. That claim came from a
mis-sourced citation, since repaired. The discourse genuinely anchors `kamma`
and `cetanā`.

## Governing Decisions

- `nidānasambhava` is rendered `where X comes from` rather than the noun pile
  `the source and origin of X`. `Nidāna` takes its recorded context rule for
  source-emphasis prose rather than the `link` default, which is for
  dependent-arising sequence analysis and reads wrongly in a question about
  origin. The verb phrasing follows rule 6 of the plain-English standard.
- `vemattatā` is rendered `the differences among kinds of X`. The term is
  ungoverned. `Diversity` and `variety` were both considered and rejected as
  more abstract than the Pali, which is simply asking how the members of a
  category differ from one another.
- `veditabba` is rendered `should be understood`. The passive is kept because
  the Pali is impersonal here and an active rewrite would have to invent a
  subject.
- `kāmaguṇa` is rendered `strands of sensuality`. Ungoverned. The discourse
  turns on the distinction between the strands and sensuality itself, so the
  two must stay verbally distinct.
- The verse at 8.10 to 8.14 renders `saṅkapparāga` as `the passion in a
  person's attitude`, using the governed `saṅkappa` (`attitude`) and `rāga`
  (`passion`). The point of the verse is that sensuality is in the attitude,
  not in the attractive things, so both halves have to stay visible.
- `sāmisa` and `nirāmisa` are rendered `worldly` and `unworldly`. Both are
  ungoverned. `Of the flesh` was rejected as more archaic than the plain-English
  standard allows; `with bait` and `without bait` were rejected as opaque.
- `attabhāva` is rendered `a state of being`, and `puññabhāgiya` /
  `apuññabhāgiya` as `one that shares in benefit or one that shares in harm`,
  using the governed `puñña` (`benefit`).
- `vohāravepakka` is rendered `recognition results in how things get spoken
  of`. The Pali is making a claim about speech following recognition, and a
  noun rendering such as `has expression as its result` hides that.
- The distress line at 42.2 reuses the governed wording already fixed in the
  MN 148 and SN 36.6 control records: `grieves, wears themselves out, wails,
  beats their chest and cries, and loses their bearings`.
- The dissatisfaction definition reuses the governed SN 56.11 wording,
  including `wanting something and not getting it`, which replaced `not
  getting what one wants` across four surfaces while this discourse was being
  translated.

## Editorial Presentation

- The source states the six-aspect frame in full for `kāma` and `dukkha` and
  abridges it with `…pe…` for the four topics in between. It also abridges the
  eight path factors after their first appearance. This edition expands the
  path factors at every occurrence, because they are short and the repetition
  is the structure. It keeps a shortened `and so on through to` form for the
  four middle topic statements, which mirrors the source's own abridgment
  without leaving an untranslated ellipsis on the page.
- The translation is divided into eight sections: `The Setting`, `What the
  Exposition Covers`, and one for each of the six topics.

## Re-audit Calls

- `vemattatā`, `kāmaguṇa`, `attabhāva`, `sāmisa`, `nirāmisa`, and `vohāra` are
  all ungoverned and all recur elsewhere in the canon. If a second surface
  needs any of them, it should get a record rather than a second local
  solution.
- The five destinations (`niraya`, `tiracchānayoni`, `pettivisaya`,
  `manussaloka`, `devaloka`) are rendered plainly here and are ungoverned as a
  set. They appear twice in this discourse alone, once for the outflows and
  once for action, so the set is a reasonable candidate for a formula record.
- `nibbedhika` is ungoverned. It is rendered `penetrating` here, and the
  compound `nibbedhikapariyāya` as `penetrating exposition`. `Pariyāya` is
  governed as `mode of exposition`; the shorter `exposition` is used in the
  compound to keep the title readable.

## Practice Clarifications

- The frame is the teaching, not the six topics. Each topic is taken through
  the same six questions, and five of the six turn out to have contact as
  their source and the quenching of contact as their quenching. Only the
  outflows and dissatisfaction differ, tracing to ignorance and to ignorant
  wanting.
- The definition of action as intention is doing real work in this structure.
  It is what allows action to have a source, a set of differences, a result,
  and a quenching, in the same way that felt experience and recognition do.
- The result of dissatisfaction is unusual in being a fork rather than a
  single outcome: bewilderment or searching. The discourse does not treat
  dissatisfaction as automatically productive; it says it goes one of two
  ways.
