# SN 12.20: Paccaya Sutta (Conditions)

## Editorial Note

- Companion translator notes:
  [SN 12.20 Translation Notes](sn12-20-paccaya-sutta-notes.md)
- Primary source: the Mahāsaṅgīti Pali text in SuttaCentral's Bilara data.
- The discourse separates two things that are easy to run together: dependent
  arising, the pattern by which one thing conditions another, and the things
  that arise dependently, which are the changing items the pattern runs
  through.
- The twelve links keep the repository's governed renderings: `ignorance`,
  `putting things together`, `knowing`, `name-and-form`, `six fields of
  experience`, `contact`, `felt experience`, `ignorant wanting`, `taking
  personally`, `becoming`, `birth`, and `ageing and dying`.
- `idappaccayatā` is `conditionality`, following MN 26 and DN 15.
- The source states the full frame for the first link and again for the last,
  and abbreviates the ten between them. This edition keeps both full
  statements and names every link once in between, rather than printing the
  same paragraph twelve times.

## Translation

### What Dependent Arising Is

At Sāvatthī, the Buddha said:

"Bhikkhus, I will teach you dependent arising, and I will teach you the things
that arise dependently. Listen and pay close attention. I will speak."

"Yes, Bhante," the bhikkhus replied.

The Buddha said this:

"And what is dependent arising?

With birth as condition, bhikkhus, ageing and dying.

Whether Tathāgatas arise or not, that element still stands: the stability of
the pattern, the regularity of the pattern, conditionality. A Tathāgata
awakens to this and breaks through to it. Once he has, he tells it, teaches
it, makes it known, sets it out, reveals it, breaks it down, and makes it
plain. And he says: 'Look—with birth as condition, ageing and dying.'

The same holds at every other step:

- with becoming as condition, birth
- with taking personally as condition, becoming
- with ignorant wanting as condition, taking personally
- with felt experience as condition, ignorant wanting
- with contact as condition, felt experience
- with the six fields of experience as condition, contact
- with name-and-form as condition, the six fields of experience
- with knowing as condition, name-and-form
- with putting things together as condition, knowing
- with ignorance as condition, putting things together

Of each one of these it is the same. Whether Tathāgatas arise or not, that
element still stands: the stability of the pattern, the regularity of the
pattern, conditionality. A Tathāgata awakens to it and breaks through to it.
Once he has, he tells it, teaches it, makes it known, sets it out, reveals it,
breaks it down, and makes it plain. And he says: 'Look—with ignorance as
condition, putting things together.'

So, bhikkhus: the suchness of it, that it is not otherwise, that it does not
become something else, this conditionality—this is what is called dependent
arising.

### What Arises Dependently

And what are the things that arise dependently?

Ageing and dying, bhikkhus, are impermanent, conditioned, dependently arisen,
bound to wear away, bound to pass away, bound to fade, bound to quench.

So is every other item in the sequence:

- birth
- becoming
- taking personally
- ignorant wanting
- felt experience
- contact
- the six fields of experience
- name-and-form
- knowing
- putting things together
- ignorance

Each of these too, bhikkhus, is impermanent, conditioned, dependently arisen,
bound to wear away, bound to pass away, bound to fade, bound to quench.

These are called the things that arise dependently.

### Nothing Left to Ask About Yourself

When a noble disciple has clearly seen, with right discernment as they have
come to be, both this dependent arising and these things that arise
dependently, then for them to run back to the past—

'Was I in the past? Was I not in the past? What was I in the past? How was I
in the past? Having been what, what did I become in the past?'

—or run ahead to the future—

'Will I be in the future? Will I not be in the future? What will I be in the
future? How will I be in the future? Having been what, what will I become in
the future?'

—or be inwardly unsure about the present—

'Am I? Am I not? What am I? How am I? Where has this being come from, and
where will it go?'

—that is impossible.

Why is that? Because a noble disciple has clearly seen, with right discernment
as they have come to be, both this dependent arising and these things that
arise dependently."

The tenth discourse is finished.
