# SN 12.15: Kaccānagotta Sutta

## Editorial Note

- This is a repo-native study translation of SN 12.15, designed to be
  readable as a single Markdown document.
- Companion translator notes:
  [SN 12.15 Translation Notes](sn12-15-kaccanagotta-sutta-notes.md)
- Source basis: the Pali text of SN 12.15 as segmented in SuttaCentral's
  Bilara data.
- House choices that most affect this sutta: `sammādiṭṭhi` is rendered `right
  view`; `atthitā` is rendered `the view of existence`; `natthitā` is rendered
  `the view of non-existence`; `upādāna` is rendered `taking personally`;
  `anusaya` is rendered `underlying tendency`; `avijjā` is rendered
  `ignorance`; `saṅkhārā` is rendered `putting things together`; `viññāṇa` is
  rendered `knowing`; `paññā` is rendered `discernment`.
- This edition treats SN 12.15 as the repository's control surface for the
  middle-way definition of right view — avoiding the two extremes of "all
  exists" (eternalism) and "all does not exist" (annihilationism) — and for
  the foundational formulation that one who truly sees the arising and cessation
  of the world with right discernment is released from both extremes without
  remainder.

## Translation

### The Setting

At Sāvatthī.

Then Venerable Kaccānagotta went to the Buddha, bowed, sat to one side,
and said: "Bhante, they speak of 'right view, right view.' How far does right
view extend?"

### Right View

"Kaccāna, this world mostly relies on two opposing views: that things exist and
that they do not. But for someone who truly sees the arising of the world with right
discernment, the view of non-existence regarding the world does not occur. And
for someone who truly sees the cessation of the world with right discernment,
the view of existence regarding the world does not occur.

This world is for the most part bound by approach, taking personally, and
insistence. But someone who — with regard to all that approach, taking
personally, fixation of the heart, insistence, and underlying tendency —
does not approach, does not take personally, does not fixate on 'my self': they
have no doubt or uncertainty. What arises is just suffering arising; what
ceases is just suffering ceasing. Their knowledge of this is independent of
others.

This far, Kaccāna, is right view.

### The Two Extremes and the Middle

'All exists' — this is one extreme. 'All does not exist' — this is the second
extreme. Without approaching either of these two extremes, the Tathāgata
teaches the Dhamma by the middle:

Ignorance is the condition for putting things together.
Putting things together is the condition for knowing.
Knowing is the condition for name-and-form.
Name-and-form is the condition for the six sense fields.
The six sense fields are the condition for contact.
Contact is the condition for felt experience.
Felt experience is the condition for ignorant wanting.
Ignorant wanting is the condition for taking personally.
Taking personally is the condition for becoming.
Becoming is the condition for birth.
Birth is the condition for ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain,
distress, and despair to arise.
This is how this entire mass of dissatisfaction originates.

With the remainderless fading and cessation of ignorance, putting things
together ceases. With the cessation of putting things together, knowing ceases.
With the cessation of knowing, name-and-form ceases. With the cessation of
name-and-form, the six sense fields cease. With the cessation of the six sense
fields, contact ceases. With the cessation of contact, felt experience ceases.
With the cessation of felt experience, ignorant wanting ceases. With the
cessation of ignorant wanting, taking personally ceases. With the cessation of
taking personally, becoming ceases. With the cessation of becoming, birth
ceases. With the cessation of birth, ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation,
pain, distress, and despair cease.
This is how this entire mass of dissatisfaction ceases.

The fifth discourse is finished.
