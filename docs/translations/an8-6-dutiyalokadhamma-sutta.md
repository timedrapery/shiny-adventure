# AN 8.6: Dutiyalokadhamma Sutta

## Editorial Note

- Companion translator notes:
  [AN 8.6 Translation Notes](an8-6-dutiyalokadhamma-sutta-notes.md)
- Primary source: the Mahāsaṅgīti Pali text in SuttaCentral's Bilara data.
- The eight worldly conditions are `gain and loss, fame and disrepute, blame
  and praise, pleasure and pain`.
- `anicca`, `dukkha`, and `vipariṇāmadhamma` are rendered as `impermanent,
  dissatisfying, and bound to change` in the repeated review.
- The Pali's abbreviated repetitions are expanded for all eight conditions.

## Translation

### Eight Conditions That Turn the World

"Bhikkhus, eight worldly conditions turn around the world, and the world turns
around these eight conditions.

Which eight?

Gain and loss, fame and disrepute, blame and praise, pleasure and pain.

These eight worldly conditions turn around the world, and the world turns
around them.

### What Makes the Difference

Gain and loss, fame and disrepute, blame and praise, pleasure and pain all
happen to an unlearned ordinary person. They also happen to a learned noble
disciple.

So what is the distinction? What makes the difference between them?"

"Bhante, our teachings are rooted in the Buddha, guided by the Buddha, and take
the Buddha as their refuge. It would be good if the Buddha explained the
meaning. The bhikkhus will remember it."

"Then listen and pay close attention. I will speak."

"Yes, Bhante," they replied.

### When the Conditions Take Over

"When gain comes to an unlearned ordinary person, they do not reflect:

'This gain has come to me. It is impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to
change.'

They do not understand it as it really is.

When loss comes, they do not reflect: 'This loss has come to me. It is
impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When fame comes, they do not reflect: 'This fame has come to me. It is
impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When disrepute comes, they do not reflect: 'This disrepute has come to me. It
is impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When blame comes, they do not reflect: 'This blame has come to me. It is
impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When praise comes, they do not reflect: 'This praise has come to me. It is
impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When pleasure comes, they do not reflect: 'This pleasure has come to me. It is
impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When pain comes, they do not reflect: 'This pain has come to me. It is
impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

They do not understand it as it really is.

Gain takes over their heart. Loss takes over their heart. Fame takes over their
heart. Disrepute takes over their heart. Blame takes over their heart. Praise
takes over their heart. Pleasure takes over their heart. Pain takes over their
heart.

They welcome gain and resist loss. They welcome fame and resist disrepute. They
welcome praise and resist blame. They welcome pleasure and resist pain.

Caught in welcoming and resisting like this, they are not freed from birth,
aging, death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness, and distress. I say they are
not freed from dissatisfaction.

### When the Conditions Do Not Take Over

When gain comes to a learned noble disciple, they reflect:

'This gain has come to me. It is impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to
change.'

They understand it as it really is.

When loss comes, they reflect: 'This loss has come to me. It is impermanent,
dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When fame comes, they reflect: 'This fame has come to me. It is impermanent,
dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When disrepute comes, they reflect: 'This disrepute has come to me. It is
impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When blame comes, they reflect: 'This blame has come to me. It is impermanent,
dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When praise comes, they reflect: 'This praise has come to me. It is
impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When pleasure comes, they reflect: 'This pleasure has come to me. It is
impermanent, dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

When pain comes, they reflect: 'This pain has come to me. It is impermanent,
dissatisfying, and bound to change.'

They understand it as it really is.

Gain does not take over their heart. Loss does not take over their heart. Fame
does not take over their heart. Disrepute does not take over their heart. Blame
does not take over their heart. Praise does not take over their heart. Pleasure
does not take over their heart. Pain does not take over their heart.

They do not welcome gain or resist loss. They do not welcome fame or resist
disrepute. They do not welcome praise or resist blame. They do not welcome
pleasure or resist pain.

They give up welcoming and resisting. This frees them from birth, aging,
death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness, and distress. I say they are freed
from dissatisfaction.

This is the distinction. This is what makes the difference between a learned
noble disciple and an unlearned ordinary person.

### The Verses

Gain and loss, fame and disrepute,
blame and praise, pleasure and pain:
these conditions among human beings are impermanent,
unstable, and bound to change.

A wise person knows this and remembers.
They watch these changing conditions.
What they like does not shake their heart,
and what they dislike brings no resistance.

Their welcoming and resisting have been scattered,
have ended, and are no more.
Knowing the stainless, sorrowless state,
they understand rightly and have crossed beyond becoming."

The sixth discourse is finished.
