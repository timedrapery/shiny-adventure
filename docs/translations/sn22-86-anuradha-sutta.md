## Translation

### The Wanderers' Question

At one time the Buddha was staying near Vesālī, in the Great Wood, at the Hall
with the Peaked Roof.

At that time Venerable Anurādha was staying not far from the Buddha, in a hut
in the wilderness.

Several wandering ascetics from other traditions went to Anurādha. They
exchanged greetings and polite conversation with him, then sat to one side
and said:

"Friend Anurādha, how does the Tathāgata describe someone who is a
Tathāgata—the highest kind of person, a supreme person who has reached the
highest attainment? Does he describe them in one of these four ways?

The Tathāgata exists after death.

The Tathāgata does not exist after death.

The Tathāgata both exists and does not exist after death.

The Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist after death."

Anurādha replied:

"Friends, the Tathāgata describes someone who is a Tathāgata—the highest kind
of person, a supreme person who has reached the highest attainment—apart from
these four positions:

'The Tathāgata exists after death.'

'The Tathāgata does not exist after death.'

'The Tathāgata both exists and does not exist after death.'

'The Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist after death.'"

The wanderers said:

"This bhikkhu must be new and recently gone forth—or else he is a senior
bhikkhu who is foolish and incompetent."

They put Anurādha down by calling him new and foolish. Then they got up and
left.

### Anurādha Has Second Thoughts

Soon after they left, Anurādha thought:

"If those wanderers question me further, how should I answer so that I repeat
what the Buddha has said and do not falsely misrepresent him? How should I
explain in line with the Dhamma, so that no one has sound grounds to criticize
what I say?"

Anurādha went to the Buddha, bowed, sat to one side, and told him what had
happened.

### What Is Fit to Call Self?

"What do you think, Anurādha? Is form permanent or impermanent?"

"Impermanent, Bhante."

"And what is impermanent: is it dissatisfaction or happiness?"

"Dissatisfaction, Bhante."

"And what is impermanent, dissatisfactory, and subject to change: is it fit to
regard it like this: 'This is mine, I am this, this is my self'?"

"No, Bhante."

"Is felt experience permanent or impermanent?"

"Impermanent, Bhante."

"And what is impermanent: is it dissatisfaction or happiness?"

"Dissatisfaction, Bhante."

"And what is impermanent, dissatisfactory, and subject to change: is it fit to
regard it like this: 'This is mine, I am this, this is my self'?"

"No, Bhante."

"Is recognition permanent or impermanent?"

"Impermanent, Bhante."

"And what is impermanent: is it dissatisfaction or happiness?"

"Dissatisfaction, Bhante."

"And what is impermanent, dissatisfactory, and subject to change: is it fit to
regard it like this: 'This is mine, I am this, this is my self'?"

"No, Bhante."

"Are putting-together activities permanent or impermanent?"

"Impermanent, Bhante."

"And what is impermanent: is it dissatisfaction or happiness?"

"Dissatisfaction, Bhante."

"And what is impermanent, dissatisfactory, and subject to change: is it fit to
regard it like this: 'This is mine, I am this, this is my self'?"

"No, Bhante."

"Is knowing permanent or impermanent?"

"Impermanent, Bhante."

"And what is impermanent: is it dissatisfaction or happiness?"

"Dissatisfaction, Bhante."

"And what is impermanent, dissatisfactory, and subject to change: is it fit to
regard it like this: 'This is mine, I am this, this is my self'?"

"No, Bhante."

### Seeing the Five Heaps Clearly

"So then, Anurādha, any kind of form there is, past, future, or present,
internal or external, coarse or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near:
all form should be seen with right discernment as it has come to be: 'This is
not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.'

Any kind of felt experience there is, past, future, or present, internal or
external, coarse or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near: all felt
experience should be seen with right discernment as it has come to be: 'This
is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.'

Any kind of recognition there is, past, future, or present, internal or
external, coarse or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near: all recognition
should be seen with right discernment as it has come to be: 'This is not mine,
I am not this, this is not my self.'

Any kind of putting-together activities there are, past, future, or present,
internal or external, coarse or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near: all
putting-together activities should be seen with right discernment as they have
come to be: 'This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.'

Any kind of knowing there is, past, future, or present, internal or external,
coarse or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near: all knowing should be
seen with right discernment as it has come to be: 'This is not mine, I am not
this, this is not my self.'

Seeing this, Anurādha, a learned noble disciple becomes disenchanted with
form, felt experience, recognition, putting-together activities, and knowing.

Being disenchanted, they fade. Through fading, they are released.

When it is released, there is the knowledge: 'It is released.'

They discern: 'Birth is worn away, the spiritual life has been lived, what had
to be done has been done, there is no more of this state of being.'"

### Can You Pin Down the Tathāgata?

"What do you think, Anurādha? Do you regard form as the Tathāgata?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard felt experience as the Tathāgata?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard recognition as the Tathāgata?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard putting-together activities as the Tathāgata?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard knowing as the Tathāgata?"

"No, Bhante."

"What do you think, Anurādha? Do you regard the Tathāgata as being in form?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard the Tathāgata as apart from form?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard the Tathāgata as being in felt experience?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard the Tathāgata as apart from felt experience?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard the Tathāgata as being in recognition?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard the Tathāgata as apart from recognition?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard the Tathāgata as being in putting-together activities?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard the Tathāgata as apart from putting-together activities?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard the Tathāgata as being in knowing?"

"No, Bhante."

"Do you regard the Tathāgata as apart from knowing?"

"No, Bhante."

"What do you think, Anurādha? Do you regard form, felt experience,
recognition, putting-together activities, and knowing taken together as the
Tathāgata?"

"No, Bhante."

"What do you think, Anurādha? Do you regard the Tathāgata as someone who has
no form, no felt experience, no recognition, no putting-together activities,
and no knowing?"

"No, Bhante."

### What the Buddha Makes Known

"So, Anurādha, since you cannot pin down the Tathāgata as something true and
real even here and now, is it right for you to declare:

'Friends, the Tathāgata describes someone who is a Tathāgata—the highest kind
of person, a supreme person who has reached the highest attainment—apart from
these four positions: the Tathāgata exists after death; does not exist after
death; both exists and does not exist after death; or neither exists nor does
not exist after death'?"

"No, Bhante."

"Good, good, Anurādha. In the past as now, I describe only
dissatisfaction and the ending of dissatisfaction."

The fourth discourse is finished.
