## Translation

### The Setting

At one time the Buddha was staying near Sāvatthī in Jeta's Grove,
Anāthapiṇḍika's monastery.

### Sāti's Wrong View

Now at that time a bhikkhu called Sāti, the fisherman's son, had developed the
following pernicious view: "As I understand the Buddha's teaching, it is this
very knowing that roams and transmigrates, not another."

Several bhikkhus heard this rumour. They went to the bhikkhu Sāti and said:
"Is it really true, Reverend Sāti, that you hold this pernicious view: 'As I
understand the Buddha's teaching, it is this very knowing that roams and
transmigrates, not another'?"

"It is exactly so, reverends. As I understand the Buddha's teaching, it is this
very knowing that roams and transmigrates, not another."

Then, wishing to dissuade the bhikkhu Sāti from this pernicious view, those
bhikkhus pressed him, challenged him, and argued with him: "Do not say that,
Reverend Sāti. Do not misrepresent the Buddha — misrepresenting the
Buddha is not good, and the Buddha would not say that. In many ways,
Reverend Sāti, the Buddha has said that knowing is dependently arisen.
Without a condition there is no arising of knowing."

But even when pressed, challenged, and argued with in this way, the bhikkhu
Sāti held to that pernicious view stubbornly, insisting on it: "It is exactly
so, reverends. As I understand the Buddha's teaching, it is this very knowing
that roams and transmigrates, not another."

When those bhikkhus were unable to dissuade the bhikkhu Sāti from that
pernicious view, they went to the Buddha, bowed, sat to one side, and
told him what had happened: "Bhante, a bhikkhu called Sāti, the fisherman's
son, has developed this pernicious view: 'As I understand the Buddha's
teaching, it is this very knowing that roams and transmigrates, not another.'
We went to him and said: 'Do not say that, Reverend Sāti. Do not
misrepresent the Buddha — misrepresenting the Buddha is not good,
and the Buddha would not say that. In many ways, Reverend Sāti, the
Buddha has said that knowing is dependently arisen. Without a condition
there is no arising of knowing.' But even when pressed in this way, Reverend
Sāti held to that pernicious view stubbornly, insisting on it. Since we were
unable to dissuade him, we are reporting this matter to the Buddha."

Then the Buddha addressed a certain bhikkhu: "Come, bhikkhu. In my name,
tell the bhikkhu Sāti that the teacher summons him."

"Yes, Bhante," that bhikkhu replied. He went to the bhikkhu Sāti and said:
"Reverend Sāti, the teacher summons you."

"Yes, reverend," the bhikkhu Sāti replied. He went to the Buddha, bowed,
and sat to one side. The Buddha said to him: "Is it really true, Sāti,
that you hold this pernicious view: 'As I understand the Buddha's teaching, it
is this very knowing that roams and transmigrates, not another'?"

"It is exactly so, Bhante. As I understand the Buddha's teaching, it is this
very knowing that roams and transmigrates, not another."

"What knowing, Sāti?"

"Bhante, he is the one who speaks and knows, who experiences the results of
wholesome and unwholesome actions here and there."

"Misguided man, who on earth have you ever known me to teach in that way?
Misguided man, have I not said in many ways that knowing is dependently arisen?
Without a condition there is no arising of knowing. But you, misguided man, by
your wrong grasp, misrepresent me, harm yourself, and accumulate much
demerit. That will be for your long-lasting harm and suffering."

Then the Buddha said to the bhikkhus: "What do you think, bhikkhus? Has
this bhikkhu Sāti kindled even a spark of warmth in this teaching and
training?"

"How could he, Bhante? He has not."

When this was said, the bhikkhu Sāti sat silent, abashed, shoulders drooping,
downcast, dejected, unable to respond.

Knowing this, the Buddha said to him: "Misguided man, you will be known
by your own pernicious view. I shall now question the bhikkhus about this."

### Knowing Named by Its Condition

Then the Buddha said to the bhikkhus: "Bhikkhus, do you understand my
teaching as this bhikkhu Sāti does — when he misrepresents me by his wrong
grasp, harms himself, and accumulates much demerit?"

"No, Bhante. In many ways the Buddha has told us that knowing is
dependently arisen. Without a condition there is no arising of knowing."

"Good, good, bhikkhus! It is good that you understand my teaching in this way.
In many ways I have told you that knowing is dependently arisen. Without a
condition there is no arising of knowing. But still this bhikkhu Sāti
misrepresents me by his wrong grasp, harms himself, and accumulates much
demerit. That will be for his long-lasting harm and suffering.

Bhikkhus, whatever knowing arises in dependence upon a condition, it is
reckoned by that very condition. Knowing that arises in dependence on eye and
sights is reckoned as eye-knowing. Knowing that arises in dependence on ear
and sounds is reckoned as ear-knowing. Knowing that arises in dependence on
nose and smells is reckoned as nose-knowing. Knowing that arises in dependence
on tongue and tastes is reckoned as tongue-knowing. Knowing that arises in
dependence on body and touches is reckoned as body-knowing. Knowing that arises
in dependence on thinking mind and ideas is reckoned as mind-knowing.

It is like fire. Whatever fire burns in dependence upon a condition, it is
reckoned by that very condition. A fire that burns in dependence on logs is
reckoned as a log-fire. A fire that burns in dependence on kindling-chips is
reckoned as a kindling-chip-fire. A fire that burns in dependence on grass is
reckoned as a grass-fire. A fire that burns in dependence on cow-dung is
reckoned as a cow-dung-fire. A fire that burns in dependence on husks is
reckoned as a husk-fire. A fire that burns in dependence on rubbish is reckoned
as a rubbish-fire.

In the same way, bhikkhus, whatever knowing arises in dependence upon a
condition, it is reckoned by that very condition. Knowing that arises in
dependence on eye and sights is reckoned as eye-knowing. Knowing that arises in
dependence on ear and sounds is reckoned as ear-knowing. Knowing that arises in
dependence on nose and smells is reckoned as nose-knowing. Knowing that arises
in dependence on tongue and tastes is reckoned as tongue-knowing. Knowing that
arises in dependence on body and touches is reckoned as body-knowing. Knowing
that arises in dependence on thinking mind and ideas is reckoned as
mind-knowing.

### What Has Come to Be

Do you see, bhikkhus, that this has come to be?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Do you see that it arose from nutriment?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Do you see that when that nutriment ceases, what has come to be is subject to
cessation?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Does doubt arise in someone who is uncertain whether this has come to be?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Does doubt arise in someone who is uncertain whether it arose from nutriment?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Does doubt arise in someone who is uncertain whether, when that nutriment
ceases, what has come to be is subject to cessation?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Is doubt given up in someone who sees clearly with right discernment that this
has come to be?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Is doubt given up in someone who sees clearly with right discernment that it
arose from nutriment?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Is doubt given up in someone who sees clearly with right discernment that when
that nutriment ceases, what has come to be is subject to cessation?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Are you without doubt, bhikkhus, as to whether this has come to be?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Are you without doubt as to whether it arose from nutriment?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Are you without doubt as to whether, when that nutriment ceases, what has
come to be is subject to cessation?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Have you truly seen clearly with right discernment that this has come to be?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Have you truly seen clearly with right discernment that it arose from
nutriment?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Have you truly seen clearly with right discernment that when that nutriment
ceases, what has come to be is subject to cessation?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Bhikkhus, if you were to cling to, dally with, treasure, and treat as your
own this view — so pure, so bright as it is — would you understand the teaching
shown as like a raft, for crossing over, not for holding on?"

"No, Bhante."

"And if you were not to cling to, dally with, treasure, or treat as your own
this view — so pure, so bright as it is — would you understand the teaching
shown as like a raft, for crossing over, not for holding on?"

"Yes, Bhante."

### The Four Nutriments

"Bhikkhus, there are four nutriments that sustain beings that have come to be
and support those seeking to come to be. What four? Edible-food nutriment,
whether coarse or subtle; contact nutriment is the second; mental-intention
nutriment is the third; knowing nutriment is the fourth.

### The Arising Chain

And these four nutriments, bhikkhus — what is their source? What is their
origin? What are they born from? What produces them?

Ignorant wanting is their source, ignorant wanting is their origin; they are
born from ignorant wanting, and ignorant wanting produces them.

And this ignorant wanting, bhikkhus — what is its source? What is its origin?
What is it born from? What produces it?

Felt experience is its source, felt experience is its origin; it is born from
felt experience, and felt experience produces it.

And this felt experience, bhikkhus — what is its source? What is its origin?
What is it born from? What produces it?

Contact is its source, contact is its origin; it is born from contact, and
contact produces it.

And this contact, bhikkhus — what is its source? What is its origin? What is it
born from? What produces it?

The six sense fields are its source, the six sense fields are its origin; it is
born from the six sense fields, and the six sense fields produce it.

And these six sense fields, bhikkhus — what is their source? What is their
origin? What are they born from? What produces them?

Name-and-form is their source, name-and-form is their origin; they are born
from name-and-form, and name-and-form produces them.

And this name-and-form, bhikkhus — what is its source? What is its origin? What
is it born from? What produces it?

Knowing is its source, knowing is its origin; it is born from knowing, and
knowing produces it.

And this knowing, bhikkhus — what is its source? What is its origin? What is it
born from? What produces it?

Putting things together is its source, putting things together is its origin;
it is born from putting things together, and putting things together produces
it.

And this putting things together, bhikkhus — what is its source? What is its
origin? What is it born from? What produces it?

Ignorance is its source, ignorance is its origin; it is born from ignorance,
and ignorance produces it.

So, bhikkhus:

Ignorance is the condition for putting things together.
Putting things together is the condition for knowing.
Knowing is the condition for name-and-form.
Name-and-form is the condition for the six sense fields.
The six sense fields are the condition for contact.
Contact is the condition for felt experience.
Felt experience is the condition for ignorant wanting.
Ignorant wanting is the condition for taking personally.
Taking personally is the condition for becoming.
Becoming is the condition for birth.
Birth is the condition for ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain,
distress, and despair to arise.

This is how this entire mass of dissatisfaction originates.

'Birth is the condition for ageing and death' — that is what was said.
Bhikkhus, is birth indeed the condition for ageing and death, or not?"

"Birth is the condition for ageing and death, Bhante. That is how it stands
here."

"'Becoming is the condition for birth.' Is becoming indeed the condition for
birth, or not?"

"Becoming is the condition for birth, Bhante. That is how it stands here."

"'Taking personally is the condition for becoming.' Is taking personally indeed
the condition for becoming, or not?"

"Taking personally is the condition for becoming, Bhante. That is how it stands
here."

"'Ignorant wanting is the condition for taking personally.' Is ignorant wanting
indeed the condition for taking personally, or not?"

"Ignorant wanting is the condition for taking personally, Bhante. That is how
it stands here."

"'Felt experience is the condition for ignorant wanting.' Is felt experience
indeed the condition for ignorant wanting, or not?"

"Felt experience is the condition for ignorant wanting, Bhante. That is how it
stands here."

"'Contact is the condition for felt experience.' Is contact indeed the condition
for felt experience, or not?"

"Contact is the condition for felt experience, Bhante. That is how it stands
here."

"'The six sense fields are the condition for contact.' Are the six sense fields
indeed the condition for contact, or not?"

"The six sense fields are the condition for contact, Bhante. That is how it
stands here."

"'Name-and-form is the condition for the six sense fields.' Is name-and-form
indeed the condition for the six sense fields, or not?"

"Name-and-form is the condition for the six sense fields, Bhante. That is how
it stands here."

"'Knowing is the condition for name-and-form.' Is knowing indeed the condition
for name-and-form, or not?"

"Knowing is the condition for name-and-form, Bhante. That is how it stands
here."

"'Putting things together is the condition for knowing.' Is putting things

"Putting things together is the condition for knowing, Bhante. That is how it
stands here."

"'Ignorance is the condition for putting things together.' Is ignorance indeed
the condition for putting things together, or not?"

"Ignorance is the condition for putting things together, Bhante. That is how
it stands here."

"Well said, bhikkhus! Both you and I say this. When this exists, this comes to
be; from the arising of this, this arises — that is:

Ignorance is the condition for putting things together.
Putting things together is the condition for knowing.
Knowing is the condition for name-and-form.
Name-and-form is the condition for the six sense fields.
The six sense fields are the condition for contact.
Contact is the condition for felt experience.
Felt experience is the condition for ignorant wanting.
Ignorant wanting is the condition for taking personally.
Taking personally is the condition for becoming.
Becoming is the condition for birth.
Birth is the condition for ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain,
distress, and despair to arise.

This is how this entire mass of dissatisfaction originates.

### The Cessation Chain

With the remainderless fading and cessation of ignorance, putting things
together ceases. With the cessation of putting things together, knowing ceases.
With the cessation of knowing, name-and-form ceases. With the cessation of
name-and-form, the six sense fields cease. With the cessation of the six sense
fields, contact ceases. With the cessation of contact, felt experience ceases.
With the cessation of felt experience, ignorant wanting ceases. With the
cessation of ignorant wanting, taking personally ceases. With the cessation of
taking personally, becoming ceases. With the cessation of becoming, birth
ceases. With the cessation of birth, ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation,
pain, distress, and despair cease.

This is how this entire mass of dissatisfaction ceases.

'When birth ceases, ageing and death cease' — that is what was said.
Bhikkhus, when birth ceases, do ageing and death cease, or not?"

"When birth ceases, ageing and death cease, Bhante. That is how it stands
here."

"'When becoming ceases, birth ceases.' When becoming ceases, does birth cease,
or not?"

"When becoming ceases, birth ceases, Bhante. That is how it stands here."

"'When taking personally ceases, becoming ceases.' When taking personally
ceases, does becoming cease, or not?"

"When taking personally ceases, becoming ceases, Bhante. That is how it stands
here."

"'When ignorant wanting ceases, taking personally ceases.' When ignorant
wanting ceases, does taking personally cease, or not?"

"When ignorant wanting ceases, taking personally ceases, Bhante. That is how
it stands here."

"'When felt experience ceases, ignorant wanting ceases.' When felt experience

"When felt experience ceases, ignorant wanting ceases, Bhante. That is how it
stands here."

"'When contact ceases, felt experience ceases.' When contact ceases, does felt

"When contact ceases, felt experience ceases, Bhante. That is how it stands

"'When the six sense fields cease, contact ceases.' When the six sense fields

"When the six sense fields cease, contact ceases, Bhante. That is how it stands

"'When name-and-form ceases, the six sense fields cease.' When name-and-form

"'When knowing ceases, name-and-form ceases.' When knowing ceases, does

"When knowing ceases, name-and-form ceases, Bhante. That is how it stands

"'When putting things together ceases, knowing ceases.' When putting things

"When putting things together ceases, knowing ceases, Bhante. That is how it

"'When ignorance ceases, putting things together ceases.' When ignorance

"When ignorance ceases, putting things together ceases, Bhante. That is how it

"Well said, bhikkhus! Both you and I say this. When this does not exist, this

With the cessation of ignorance, putting things together ceases.
With the cessation of putting things together, knowing ceases.
With the cessation of knowing, name-and-form ceases.
With the cessation of name-and-form, the six sense fields cease.
With the cessation of the six sense fields, contact ceases.
With the cessation of contact, felt experience ceases.
With the cessation of felt experience, ignorant wanting ceases.
With the cessation of ignorant wanting, taking personally ceases.
With the cessation of taking personally, becoming ceases.
With the cessation of becoming, birth ceases.
With the cessation of birth, ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain,

This is how this entire mass of dissatisfaction ceases.

### Known and Seen for Yourselves

Knowing and seeing in this way, bhikkhus, would you run back to the past,
wondering: 'Did we exist in past time? Did we not? What were we in past time?
How were we? Having been what, what did we become?'"

"No, Bhante."

"Would you run forward to the future, wondering: 'Will we exist in future time?
Will we not? What will we be? How will we be? Having been what, what will we
become?'"

"No, Bhante."

"Would you be uncertain about the present, wondering: 'Am I? Am I not? What
am I? How am I? This being — where has it come from? Where will it go?'"

"No, Bhante."

"Would you say: 'Our teacher is venerable — we speak out of reverence for our
teacher'?"

"No, Bhante."

"Would you say: 'The recluse says this — and we speak as we do because it is
what the recluse says'?"

"No, Bhante."

"Would you seek out another teacher?"

"No, Bhante."

"Would you return to the observances and auspicious rites of the various
recluses and brahmins as something essential?"

"No, Bhante."

"Are you not speaking only of what you have yourselves known, yourselves seen,
yourselves realized?"

"Yes, Bhante."

"Well said, bhikkhus! You have been guided by me with this teaching that is
apparent in the present life, immediately effective, inviting inspection,
leading inward — to be known for themselves by those with discernment. When I
said that this teaching is apparent in the present life, immediately effective,
inviting inspection, leading inward — to be known for themselves by those with
discernment — it is this that I was referring to.

### How a Being Is Conceived

Bhikkhus, when three things come together, there is the descent of an embryo.
Here, mother and father come together, but the mother is not in her fertile
period and the gandhabba is not present — no descent of an embryo occurs.
Here, mother and father come together and the mother is in her fertile period,
but the gandhabba is not present — no descent of an embryo occurs. But when
mother and father come together, the mother is in her fertile period, and the
gandhabba is present — these three things coming together make for the descent
of an embryo.

The mother carries that embryo in her womb for nine or ten months with great
difficulty, bearing a heavy burden. After nine or ten months, the mother gives
birth with great difficulty, bearing a heavy burden. She then nourishes the
newborn with her own blood — for in the training of the noble ones, mother's
milk is regarded as blood.

### The Ordinary Course

That boy grows up and his faculties mature. He plays the games that boys play:
toy plows, tipcat, handstands, pinwheels, toy measures, toy carts, toy bows.

That boy grows up and his faculties mature further. He amuses himself,
furnished with the five strands of sensual pleasure: sights cognized by the
eye that are desired, agreeable, pleasing, of a pleasant nature, connected to
sensual pleasure, arousing; sounds cognized by the ear... smells cognized by
the nose... tastes cognized by the tongue... touches cognized by the body that
are desired, agreeable, pleasing, of a pleasant nature, connected to sensual
pleasure, arousing.

When he sees a sight with his eyes, he is drawn toward what is pleasing and
repelled by what is displeasing. He lives with remembering of the body
unestablished, with a diminished heart. He does not truly understand
that release of mind and release through discernment where those arisen bad,
unwholesome qualities cease without remainder.

Being full of favoring and opposing in this way, whatever felt experience he
experiences — pleasant, painful, or mixed — he approves of it, welcomes it,
keeps clinging to it. From this, relishing arises. The relishing of felt
experience — that is taking personally. With taking personally as condition,
becoming; with becoming as condition, birth; with birth as condition, ageing
and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, and despair arise. This is how
this entire mass of dissatisfaction originates.

When he hears a sound with his ears... when he smells a smell with his nose...
when he tastes a taste with his tongue... when he feels a touch with his
body... when he cognizes an idea with his thinking mind, he is drawn toward
what is pleasing and repelled by what is displeasing. He lives with remembering
of the body unestablished, with a diminished heart. He does not truly
understand that release of mind and release through discernment where those
arisen bad, unwholesome qualities cease without remainder.

Being full of favoring and opposing in this way, whatever felt experience he
experiences — pleasant, painful, or mixed — he approves of it, welcomes it,
keeps clinging to it. From this, relishing arises. The relishing of felt
experience — that is taking personally. With taking personally as condition,
becoming; with becoming as condition, birth; with birth as condition, ageing
and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, and despair arise. This is how
this entire mass of dissatisfaction originates.

### The Arising of the Tathāgata

Here, bhikkhus, a Tathāgata arises in the world — an arahant, perfectly and
fully awakened, accomplished in knowledge and conduct, well-departed, knower
of the world, unsurpassed guide for those to be tamed, teacher of gods and
humans, awakened, blessed. Having by himself, with direct knowledge, realized
this world with its gods, Māras, and Brahmās, this population with its recluses
and brahmins, its gods and humans, he makes it known to others. He teaches a
Dhamma that is good in the beginning, good in the middle, and good in the end,
with meaning and with phrasing. He reveals a spiritual life that is utterly
full and utterly pure.

### Going Forth and the Training

A householder hears that Dhamma, or a householder's child, or someone born into
a family. When they hear it, they gain confidence in the Tathāgata. With that
confidence, they reflect: 'Household life is cramped and dusty; the going-forth
is wide open. It is not easy for someone living at home to live the spiritual
life perfectly full and perfectly pure, like a polished shell. Why not shave
off hair and beard, put on ochre robes, and go forth from home into
homelessness?'

At some later time they give up a small or large fortune, give up a small or
large circle of kin, shave off hair and beard, put on ochre robes, and go forth
from home into homelessness.

Once they have gone forth, they take up the training and livelihood of the
bhikkhus.
Abandoning the taking of life, they refrain from taking life — laying down the
rod and the sword, scrupulous, full of care, compassionate toward all living
beings. Abandoning the taking of what is not given, they refrain — taking only
what is given, expecting only what is given, they live cleanly without
stealing. Abandoning unchastity, they live the spiritual life, celibate, set
apart, refraining from the vulgar act of sex. Abandoning false speech, they
refrain — speaking the truth, holding to the truth, trustworthy, reliable, not
deceiving the world. Abandoning divisive speech, they refrain — not repeating
here what they heard there to divide people, not repeating there what they
heard here to divide people. They reconcile those who are divided, unite those
who are united, delighting in harmony, loving harmony, speaking words that
promote harmony. Abandoning harsh speech, they refrain — they speak in a way
that is gentle, pleasing to the ear, endearing, going to the heart, gracious,
agreeable and likable to many. Abandoning idle chatter, they refrain — they
speak at the right time, speak what is true, speak what is beneficial, speak
the Dhamma, speak the training; at the right time they say things that are
worth treasuring, well-reasoned, clearly defined, connected with what is
beneficial.

They refrain from injuring plant life and seeds. They eat at one sitting, not
at night, refraining from food at the wrong time. They refrain from attending
shows of dancing, singing, and music. They refrain from adorning and
beautifying themselves with garlands, fragrance, and cosmetics. They refrain
from high and luxurious beds. They refrain from accepting gold and silver, raw
grain, raw meat, women and girls, male and female servants, goats and sheep,
chickens and pigs, elephants, cattle, horses, and mares, and fields and land.
They refrain from running errands and carrying messages; from buying and
selling; from cheating with false weights, metals, and measures; from bribery,
fraud, deception, and duplicity; from mutilation, murder, abduction, banditry,
plunder, and violence.

They are content with robes to protect the body and almsfood to protect the
belly. Wherever they go they set out taking only these. Like a bird that flies
wherever it goes bearing only its wings as its burden — in the same way, a
bhikkhu is content with robes to protect the body and almsfood to protect the
belly, and wherever they go they set out taking only these.

Possessed of this entire noble aggregate of conduct, they experience within
themselves a blameless satisfaction.

### Sense Restraint and Clear Knowing

When they see a sight with their eyes, they do not grasp at its features or
its details. Since, if the faculty of sight were left unrestrained, bad
unwholesome qualities of coveting and distress would overwhelm them, they
practice restraint over it — they guard the faculty of sight, they arrive at
restraint of the faculty of sight.

When they hear a sound with their ears... when they smell a smell with their
nose... when they taste a taste with their tongue... when they feel a touch
with their body... when they cognize an idea with their thinking mind, they do
not grasp at its features or its details. Since, if the faculty of thinking
mind were left unrestrained, bad unwholesome qualities of coveting and distress
would overwhelm them, they practice restraint over it — they guard the faculty
of thinking mind, they arrive at restraint of the faculty of thinking mind.

Possessed of this noble restraint of the sense faculties, they experience
within themselves an unsullied satisfaction.

They act with clear knowing when going forward and returning; when looking
ahead and aside; when bending and extending the limbs; when wearing the outer
robe, bowl, and robes; when eating, drinking, chewing, and tasting; when
urinating and defecating; when walking, standing, sitting, sleeping, waking,
speaking, and keeping silent.

Possessed of this entire noble aggregate of conduct, this noble contentment,
this noble restraint of the sense faculties, and this noble remembering and
clear knowing, they resort to a secluded dwelling — a wilderness, the foot of
a tree, a hill, a ravine, a mountain cave, a charnel ground, a forest, the
open air, a heap of straw.

### Abandoning the Distractions and Entering the Mental Themes

Returning from the alms-round after the meal, they sit down cross-legged, hold
their body erect, and establish remembering in front.

Abandoning coveting for the world, they live with a heart rid of
coveting, cleansing the heart of coveting. Abandoning aversive
distraction and malevolence, they live with a heart free of aversive
distraction, full of compassion toward all living beings, cleansing the heart
of aversive distraction. Abandoning dullness and drowsiness, they live
rid of dullness and drowsiness, perceiving light, remembering and clearly
knowing, cleansing the heart of dullness and drowsiness. Abandoning
restlessness and remorse, they live unrestless, with the heart inwardly
at peace, cleansing the heart of restlessness and remorse. Abandoning
uncertain distraction, they have crossed beyond uncertainty. They are no
longer undecided about wholesome qualities, and they cleanse the heart
of uncertain distraction.

They have abandoned these five distractions, the corruptions of the heart
that weaken discernment. Then, secluded from sense pleasures, secluded
from unwholesome qualities, they enter and remain in the first mental theme —
with placing the mind, keeping it there — with rejoicing and satisfaction born
of seclusion.

With the settling of placing and keeping the mind, internally confident, with
a unified heart, without placing and keeping the mind, they enter and
remain in the second mental theme with rejoicing and satisfaction born of
composure.

With the fading of rejoicing they remain equanimous, remembering and clearly
knowing, and experience satisfaction with the body — what the noble ones
describe as 'equanimous, remembering, living well' — and they enter and remain
in the third mental theme.

With the abandoning of satisfaction and distress, and with the prior ending of
gladness and grief, they enter and remain in the fourth mental theme — neither
satisfaction nor distress, with purity of equanimity and remembering.

### Freedom Through the Ending of Craving

When they see a sight with their eyes, they are not drawn toward what is
pleasing and not repelled by what is displeasing. They live with remembering
of the body established, with a boundless heart. They truly understand
that release of mind and release through discernment where those arisen bad,
unwholesome qualities cease without remainder.

Once they have given up favoring and opposing in this way, whatever felt
experience they experience — pleasant, painful, or mixed — they do not approve
of it, do not welcome it, do not keep clinging to it. As a result, the
relishing of felt experience ceases. With the cessation of relishing, taking
personally ceases; with the cessation of taking personally, becoming ceases;
with the cessation of becoming, birth ceases; with the cessation of birth,
ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, and despair cease. This
is how this entire mass of dissatisfaction ceases.

When they hear a sound with their ears... when they smell a smell with their
nose... when they taste a taste with their tongue... when they feel a touch
with their body... when they cognize an idea with their thinking mind, they are
not drawn toward what is pleasing and not repelled by what is displeasing. They
live with remembering of the body established, with a boundless heart.
They truly understand that release of mind and release through discernment where
those arisen bad, unwholesome qualities cease without remainder.

Once they have given up favoring and opposing in this way, whatever felt
experience they experience — pleasant, painful, or mixed — they do not approve
of it, do not welcome it, do not keep clinging to it. As a result, the
relishing of felt experience ceases. With the cessation of relishing, taking
personally ceases; with the cessation of taking personally, becoming ceases;
with the cessation of becoming, birth ceases; with the cessation of birth,
ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, and despair cease. This
is how this entire mass of dissatisfaction ceases.

Bhikkhus, hold this brief statement on freedom through the ending of ignorant
wanting. But the bhikkhu Sāti, the fisherman's son, is caught up in a vast net
of ignorant wanting, entangled in a tangle of ignorant wanting."

That is what the Buddha said. Satisfied, those bhikkhus approved of the
Buddha's words.

The eighth discourse is finished.
