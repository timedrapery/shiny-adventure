# MN 122: Mahāsuññata Sutta (The Longer Discourse on Emptiness)

## Editorial Note

## Translation

### The Setting

So I heard.

At one time the Buddha was staying among the Sakyans at Kapilavatthu, in the
Nigrodha grove.

Then, in the morning, the Buddha robed up and, taking bowl and robe, went
into Kapilavatthu for alms. After walking for alms in Kapilavatthu, he
returned from his meal and went to the dwelling of Kāḷakhemaka the Sakyan to
spend the day. At that time many lodgings had been set out in Kāḷakhemaka's
dwelling. The Buddha saw them, and thought: "Many lodgings have been set out
in Kāḷakhemaka's dwelling. Are many bhikkhus staying here?"

At that time Venerable Ānanda, together with many bhikkhus, was making robes
at the dwelling of Ghaṭāya the Sakyan. In the late afternoon the Buddha came
out of retreat and went to Ghaṭāya's dwelling, where he sat on the seat set
out for him and said to Venerable Ānanda:

"Ānanda, many lodgings have been set out in Kāḷakhemaka's dwelling. Are many
bhikkhus staying there?"

"Yes, Bhante, many lodgings have been set out there, and many bhikkhus are
staying there. It is our time for making robes."

### Delight in Company

"Ānanda, a bhikkhu does not shine who delights in company, loves company, and
is given to delighting in company, who delights in a group, loves a group, and
is pleased with a group.

That a bhikkhu who delights in company, loves company, and is given to
delighting in company, who delights in a group, loves a group, and is pleased
with a group, will gain at will, without trouble or difficulty, the
satisfaction of renunciation, the satisfaction of deep seclusion, the
satisfaction of calming, the satisfaction of awakening: that is not possible.
But a bhikkhu who lives alone, withdrawn from the group, can expect to gain at
will, without trouble or difficulty, the satisfaction of renunciation, the
satisfaction of deep seclusion, the satisfaction of calming, the satisfaction
of awakening: that is possible.

That a bhikkhu who delights in company, loves company, and is given to
delighting in company, who delights in a group, loves a group, and is pleased
with a group, will enter and remain in a release of mind that is temporary and
delightful, or in one that is not temporary and is unshakable: that is not
possible. But a bhikkhu who lives alone, withdrawn from the group, can expect
to enter and remain in a release of mind that is temporary and delightful, or
in one that is not temporary and is unshakable: that is possible.

Ānanda, I do not see even a single form from whose change and alteration
sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, and despair would not arise for someone
who is passionate about it and delights in it.

### The Tathāgata's Way of Dwelling

But, Ānanda, the Tathāgata has fully awakened to this way of dwelling: by not
attending to any signs, he enters and remains in emptiness internally.

If, while the Tathāgata is dwelling in this way, bhikkhus, bhikkhunīs, male
and female lay followers, rulers and royal ministers, and the teachers of
other schools and their disciples come to see him, then, with a heart that
slants, slopes, and inclines toward seclusion, withdrawn, delighting in
renunciation, entirely done with the dhammas that are a basis for the
outflows, the Tathāgata always speaks to them in a way that is concerned with
sending them on their way.

### Steadying the Heart Within

So, Ānanda, if a bhikkhu should wish, 'May I enter and remain in emptiness
internally,' they should steady their heart within, settle it, make it single,
and unify it.

And how does a bhikkhu steady the heart within, settle it, make it single, and
unify it?

Ānanda, quite secluded from sensuality, secluded from unwholesome dhammas, a
bhikkhu enters and remains in the first mental theme, with thinking and
pondering, with rejoicing and satisfaction born of seclusion.

With the subsiding of thinking and pondering, with inner clarity and
singleness of mind, they enter and remain in the second mental theme, without
thinking and pondering, with rejoicing and satisfaction born of unification.

With the fading of rejoicing, they remain in dynamic balance, remembering and
clearly knowing, and they experience ease in the body. They enter and remain
in the third mental theme, of which the noble ones say: 'One of dynamic
balance and remembering stays at ease.'

With the abandoning of ease and discomfort, and with the earlier ending of
gladness and sadness, they enter and remain in the fourth mental theme,
without discomfort and without ease, purified by dynamic balance and
remembering.

Ānanda, this is how a bhikkhu steadies the heart within, settles it, makes it
single, and unifies it.

### Attending to Emptiness

They attend to emptiness internally. As they attend to emptiness internally,
their heart does not leap forward into emptiness internally, does not become
clear, does not settle, and is not released. Ānanda, when that is so, the
bhikkhu discerns: 'As I attend to emptiness internally, my heart does not leap
forward into emptiness internally, does not become clear, does not settle, and
is not released.' In this way they clearly know it.

They attend to emptiness externally. As they attend to emptiness externally,
their heart does not leap forward into emptiness externally, does not become
clear, does not settle, and is not released. When that is so, the bhikkhu
discerns: 'As I attend to emptiness externally, my heart does not leap forward
into emptiness externally, does not become clear, does not settle, and is not
released.' In this way they clearly know it.

They attend to emptiness internally and externally. As they attend to
emptiness internally and externally, their heart does not leap forward into
emptiness internally and externally, does not become clear, does not settle,
and is not released. When that is so, the bhikkhu discerns: 'As I attend to
emptiness internally and externally, my heart does not leap forward into
emptiness internally and externally, does not become clear, does not settle,
and is not released.' In this way they clearly know it.

They attend to the imperturbable. As they attend to the imperturbable, their
heart does not leap forward into the imperturbable, does not become clear, does
not settle, and is not released. Ānanda, when that is so, the bhikkhu discerns:
'As I attend to the imperturbable, my heart does not leap forward into the
imperturbable, does not become clear, does not settle, and is not released.' In
this way they clearly know it.

Then, Ānanda, that bhikkhu should steady their heart within, settle it, make
it single, and unify it in that same earlier sign of unification.

They attend to emptiness internally. As they attend to emptiness internally,
their heart leaps forward into emptiness internally, becomes clear, settles,
and is released. Ānanda, when that is so, the bhikkhu discerns: 'As I attend to
emptiness internally, my heart leaps forward into emptiness internally, becomes
clear, settles, and is released.' In this way they clearly know it.

They attend to emptiness externally. As they attend to emptiness externally,
their heart leaps forward into emptiness externally, becomes clear, settles,
and is released. When that is so, the bhikkhu discerns: 'As I attend to
emptiness externally, my heart leaps forward into emptiness externally,
becomes clear, settles, and is released.' In this way they clearly know it.

They attend to emptiness internally and externally. As they attend to
emptiness internally and externally, their heart leaps forward into emptiness
internally and externally, becomes clear, settles, and is released. When that
is so, the bhikkhu discerns: 'As I attend to emptiness internally and
externally, my heart leaps forward into emptiness internally and externally,
becomes clear, settles, and is released.' In this way they clearly know it.

They attend to the imperturbable. As they attend to the imperturbable, their
heart leaps forward into the imperturbable, becomes clear, settles, and is
released. Ānanda, when that is so, the bhikkhu discerns: 'As I attend to the
imperturbable, my heart leaps forward into the imperturbable, becomes clear,
settles, and is released.' In this way they clearly know it.

### Walking, Standing, Sitting, and Lying Down

Ānanda, if while a bhikkhu is dwelling in this way, their heart inclines to
walking, they walk, knowing: 'While I walk like this, bad, unwholesome dhammas
of coveting and distress will not flood in on me.' In this way they clearly
know it.

If, while they are dwelling in this way, their heart inclines to standing,
they stand, knowing: 'While I stand like this, bad, unwholesome dhammas of
coveting and distress will not flood in on me.' In this way they clearly know
it.

If, while they are dwelling in this way, their heart inclines to sitting, they
sit, knowing: 'While I sit like this, bad, unwholesome dhammas of coveting and
distress will not flood in on me.' In this way they clearly know it.

If, while they are dwelling in this way, their heart inclines to lying down,
they lie down, knowing: 'While I lie down like this, bad, unwholesome dhammas
of coveting and distress will not flood in on me.' In this way they clearly
know it.

### Talk

Ānanda, if while a bhikkhu is dwelling in this way, their heart inclines to
talking, they resolve: 'I will not engage in talk that is low, vulgar,
ordinary, ignoble, and unbeneficial, that does not lead to disenchantment,
fading, cessation, calming, higher knowing, awakening, or nibbāna: talk about
kings, robbers, and ministers; armies, dangers, and battles; food, drink,
clothes, and beds; garlands and scents; relatives, vehicles, villages, towns,
cities, and countries; women and heroes; the street and the well; the dead;
trivia; tales of the world and of the sea; and about being this way or that.'
In this way they clearly know it.

'But there is talk, Ānanda, that is wholly self-effacing, that helps the
heart come free of hindrances, and that leads only to disenchantment, fading,
cessation, calming, higher knowing, awakening, and nibbāna: talk on fewness of
wishes, on contentment, on deep seclusion, on non-entanglement, on arousing
energy, on conduct, on unification, on discernment, on release, and on the
knowing and seeing of release. I will engage in talk like that.' In this way
they clearly know it.

### Thinking

Ānanda, if while a bhikkhu is dwelling in this way, their heart inclines to
thinking, they resolve: 'I will not think the kinds of thinking that are low,
vulgar, ordinary, ignoble, and unbeneficial, that do not lead to
disenchantment, fading, cessation, calming, higher knowing, awakening, or
nibbāna: thinking of sensuality, thinking of ill will, and thinking of
harming.' In this way they clearly know it.

'But there are kinds of thinking, Ānanda, that are noble and lead out, and
that lead whoever acts on them to the complete ending of dissatisfaction:
thinking of renunciation, thinking of non-ill-will, and thinking of
non-harming. I will think thinking like that.' In this way they clearly know
it.

### The Five Kinds of Sensual Stimulation

Ānanda, there are these five kinds of sensual stimulation. What five?
Visible forms knowable by the eye, sounds knowable by the ear, smells knowable
by the nose, tastes knowable by the tongue, and tangibles knowable by the body
that are likable, attractive, agreeable, pleasing, tied to sensuality, and
passion stirring. These are the five kinds of sensual stimulation.

A bhikkhu should keep checking their own heart: 'Does my mind become involved
with one or another field among these five kinds of sensual stimulation?'

If, on checking, the bhikkhu discerns, 'My mind does become involved with one
or another field among these five kinds of sensual stimulation,' then, when
that is so, Ānanda, the bhikkhu discerns: 'Desire and passion for these five
kinds of sensual stimulation have not been abandoned in me.' In this way they
clearly know it.

But if, on checking, the bhikkhu discerns, 'My mind does not become involved
with any field among these five kinds of sensual stimulation,' then, when that
is so, Ānanda, the bhikkhu discerns: 'Desire and passion for these five kinds
of sensual stimulation have been abandoned in me.' In this way they clearly
know it.

### The Five Clung-to Heaps

Ānanda, there are these five clung-to heaps, in which a bhikkhu should remain
observing arising and passing away:

'Such is form. Such is the arising of form. Such is the vanishing of form.

Such is felt experience. Such is the arising of felt experience. Such is the
vanishing of felt experience.

Such is recognition. Such is the arising of recognition. Such is the
vanishing of recognition.

Such are putting-together activities. Such is the arising of putting-together
activities. Such is the vanishing of putting-together activities.

Such is knowing. Such is the arising of knowing. Such is the vanishing of
knowing.'

As they remain observing arising and passing away in these five clung-to heaps,
the conceit 'I am' in the five clung-to heaps is abandoned. Ānanda, when that
is so, the bhikkhu discerns: 'The conceit "I am" in these five clung-to heaps
has been abandoned in me.' In this way they clearly know it.

Ānanda, these dhammas are entirely wholesome, with wholesome outcomes, noble,
beyond the world, and out of the Wicked One's reach.

### Why a Disciple Follows the Teacher

What do you think, Ānanda? For what reason is a disciple worthy to follow the
teacher, even when told to go away?"

"Bhante, our teachings are rooted in the Buddha, guided by him, and have him
as their refuge. It would be good if the Buddha himself would explain the
meaning of what he has said. The bhikkhus will remember it after hearing it
from him."

"Ānanda, it is not for the sake of discourses, songs, and explanations that a
disciple is worthy to follow the teacher. Why is that? Ānanda, for a long time,
you have heard those teachings, remembered them, recited them, examined them
with the mind, and penetrated them well by view.

But there is talk, Ānanda, that is wholly self-effacing, that helps the heart
come free of hindrances, and that leads only to disenchantment, fading,
cessation, calming, higher knowing, awakening, and nibbāna: talk on fewness of
wishes, on contentment, on deep seclusion, on non-entanglement, on arousing
energy, on conduct, on unification, on discernment, on release, and on the
knowing and seeing of release. It is for the sake of talk like that, Ānanda,
that a disciple is worthy to follow the teacher, even when told to go away.

### Three Kinds of Ruin

That being so, Ānanda, there can be the ruin of a teacher, the ruin of a
pupil, and the ruin of someone who lives the spiritual life.

And how does a teacher come to ruin, Ānanda? Ānanda, some teacher resorts to a
secluded lodging: a forest, the root of a tree, a mountain, a ravine, a
hillside cave, a charnel ground, a jungle thicket, the open air, a heap of
straw. While he lives withdrawn like this, brahmins and householders from town
and country come flocking to him. When they come flocking to him, he becomes
infatuated, falls into greed, and turns back to luxury. This is called a
teacher ruined by the teacher's ruin. Bad, unwholesome dhammas strike him down:
defiling, leading to renewed becoming, troubling, ripening in dissatisfaction,
and leading to future birth, ageing, and death. This is how a teacher comes to
ruin.

And how does a pupil come to ruin, Ānanda? A disciple of that same teacher,
following the teacher's example of seclusion, resorts to a secluded lodging: a
forest, the root of a tree, a mountain, a ravine, a hillside cave, a charnel
ground, a jungle thicket, the open air, a heap of straw. While he lives
withdrawn like this, brahmins and householders from town and country come
flocking to him. When they come flocking to him, he becomes infatuated, falls
into greed, and turns back to luxury. This is called a pupil ruined by the
pupil's ruin. Bad, unwholesome dhammas strike him down: defiling, leading to
renewed becoming, troubling, ripening in dissatisfaction, and leading to future
birth, ageing, and death. This is how a pupil comes to ruin.

And how does someone who lives the spiritual life come to ruin, Ānanda? Ānanda,
a Tathāgata arises in the world, the arahant, the fully awakened Buddha,
accomplished in knowledge and conduct, holy, knower of the world, unsurpassed
guide for those fit for training, teacher of gods and humans, awakened,
blessed. He resorts to a secluded lodging: a forest, the root of a tree, a
mountain, a ravine, a hillside cave, a charnel ground, a jungle thicket, the
open air, a heap of straw. While he lives withdrawn like this, brahmins and
householders from town and country come flocking to him. When they come
flocking to him, he does not become infatuated, does not fall into greed, and
does not turn back to luxury.

But a disciple of that same teacher, following the teacher's example of
seclusion, resorts to a secluded lodging: a forest, the root of a tree, a
mountain, a ravine, a hillside cave, a charnel ground, a jungle thicket, the
open air, a heap of straw. While he lives withdrawn like this, brahmins and
householders from town and country come flocking to him. When they come
flocking to him, he becomes infatuated, falls into greed, and turns back to
luxury. This is called someone who lives the spiritual life ruined by the ruin
of the spiritual life. Bad, unwholesome dhammas strike him down: defiling,
leading to renewed becoming, troubling, ripening in dissatisfaction, and
leading to future birth, ageing, and death. This is how someone who lives the
spiritual life comes to ruin.

And here, Ānanda, the ruin of someone who lives the spiritual life has a more
painful and more bitter result than the ruin of a teacher or the ruin of a
pupil. It even leads to a downfall state.

### As a Friend, Not an Enemy

So, Ānanda, treat me as a friend, not as an enemy. That will be for your
welfare and satisfaction for a long time.

Ānanda, how do disciples treat the teacher as an enemy and not as a friend?
Ānanda, out of sympathy, the teacher teaches the disciples the Dhamma, seeking
their welfare, moved by sympathy: 'This is for your welfare. This is for your
satisfaction.' His disciples do not want to listen. They do not lend an ear,
they do not apply their hearts to understanding, and they turn away from the
teacher's instruction. This is how disciples treat the teacher as an enemy and
not as a friend.

Ānanda, how do disciples treat the teacher as a friend and not as an enemy?
Ānanda, out of sympathy, the teacher teaches the disciples the Dhamma, seeking
their welfare, moved by sympathy: 'This is for your welfare. This is for your
satisfaction.' His disciples want to listen. They lend an ear, they apply their
hearts to understanding, and they do not turn away from the teacher's
instruction. This is how disciples treat the teacher as a friend and not as an
enemy.

So, Ānanda, treat me as a friend, not as an enemy. That will be for your
welfare and satisfaction for a long time.

I will not deal with you, Ānanda, the way a potter deals with clay that is raw
and still damp. I will speak to you, Ānanda, correcting you again and again. I
will speak to you, pressing you again and again. Whatever is sound will
stand."

That is what the Buddha said. Venerable Ānanda was glad and welcomed what the
Buddha had said.

The second discourse is finished.
