## Translation

### The Bhikkhus' Conversation

So I heard. At one time the Buddha was staying near Sāvatthī, in Jeta's Grove,
Anāthapiṇḍika's monastery.

After the meal, several bhikkhus gathered in the meeting hall. This conversation
came up among them:

"It is remarkable, friends, truly wonderful, how the Buddha—who knows and
sees, the arahant, fully awakened—has said that kāyagatā sati, when developed
and cultivated, brings great fruit and great benefit."

Before they had finished, the Buddha came to the meeting hall, sat on the seat
prepared for him, and asked what they had been discussing. They told him.

"Bhikkhus, how does kāyagatā sati—sati directed to the body—bring great fruit
and great benefit when it is developed and cultivated?

### Remembering While Breathing

A bhikkhu goes to the wilderness, the root of a tree, or an empty hut. They sit
down, fold their legs crosswise, set the body upright, and set remembering to
the fore. They breathe in remembering the Dhamma; they breathe out remembering
the Dhamma.

Breathing in long, they know: 'I breathe in long.' Breathing out long, they
know: 'I breathe out long.'

Breathing in short, they know: 'I breathe in short.' Breathing out short, they
know: 'I breathe out short.'

They train: 'Breathing in, I will experience the whole body.' They train:
'Breathing out, I will experience the whole body.'

They train: 'Breathing in, I will calm the body conditioner.' They train:
'Breathing out, I will calm the body conditioner.'

As they remain diligent, ardent, and committed, thoughts tied to household life
are given up. With those thoughts gone, the heart settles within, grows quiet,
becomes unified, and is mentally composed.

This is how a bhikkhu develops kāyagatā sati.

### Postures and Ordinary Activities

Again, a bhikkhu knows 'I am walking' when walking, 'I am standing' when
standing, 'I am sitting' when sitting, and 'I am lying down' when lying down.
However the body is placed, they know it that way.

As they remain diligent, ardent, and committed, thoughts tied to household life
are given up. With those thoughts gone, the heart settles within, grows quiet,
becomes unified, and is mentally composed.

This too is how a bhikkhu develops kāyagatā sati.

Again, they act with clear knowing when going forward and coming back, looking
ahead and looking away, bending and stretching, carrying robe and bowl, eating,
drinking, chewing, and tasting, urinating and defecating, walking, standing,
sitting, falling asleep, waking, speaking, and keeping silent.

As they remain diligent, ardent, and committed, thoughts tied to household life
are given up. With those thoughts gone, the heart settles within, grows quiet,
becomes unified, and is mentally composed.

This too is how a bhikkhu develops kāyagatā sati.

### Reviewing the Body

Again, a bhikkhu reviews this very body from the soles of the feet up and from
the hair of the head down, enclosed by skin and full of many kinds of unclean
things:

'In this body there are head hairs, body hairs, nails, teeth, skin, flesh,
tendons, bones, bone marrow, kidneys, heart, liver, membranes, spleen, lungs,
intestines, mesentery, stomach contents, feces, bile, phlegm, pus, blood,
sweat, fat, tears, grease, saliva, mucus, joint-fluid, and urine.'

It is like a bag with openings at both ends, full of hill-rice, ordinary rice,
mung beans, peas, sesame seeds, and rice grains. A person with good sight could
open it and identify what is inside. In the same way, the bhikkhu reviews this
body and identifies what is inside it.

As they remain diligent, ardent, and committed, thoughts tied to household life
are given up. With those thoughts gone, the heart settles within, grows quiet,
becomes unified, and is mentally composed.

This too is how a bhikkhu develops kāyagatā sati.

### The Four Elements

Again, a bhikkhu reviews this very body, however it is placed, in terms of
elements:

'In this body there are the earth element, the water element, the fire element,
and the air element.'

It is like a skilled butcher or butcher's apprentice who has killed a cow, cut
it into portions, and sits with them at a crossroads. In the same way, the
bhikkhu reviews this body in terms of its elements.

As they remain diligent, ardent, and committed, thoughts tied to household life
are given up. With those thoughts gone, the heart settles within, grows quiet,
becomes unified, and is mentally composed.

This too is how a bhikkhu develops kāyagatā sati.

### What the Body Becomes

Again, suppose a bhikkhu were to see a corpse thrown away in a charnel ground:

- one, two, or three days dead, bloated, livid, and oozing;
- being eaten by crows, hawks, vultures, dogs, jackals, or different kinds of
  creatures;
- reduced to a skeleton with flesh and blood, held together by tendons;
- reduced to a fleshless skeleton smeared with blood, held together by tendons;
- reduced to a bloodless skeleton, held together by tendons;
- with bones disconnected and scattered in every direction—hand bones here,
  foot bones there, ankle, shin, thigh, hip, rib, spine, shoulder, neck, jaw,
  teeth, and skull elsewhere;
- bones white like shells;
- bones heaped up, more than a year old;
- bones rotten and crumbling to dust.

At each stage they apply what they see to this body: 'This body too is of the
same nature. It will become like that and has not gone beyond it.'

As they remain diligent, ardent, and committed, thoughts tied to household life
are given up. With those thoughts gone, the heart settles within, grows quiet,
becomes unified, and is mentally composed.

This too is how a bhikkhu develops kāyagatā sati.

### The First Mental Theme

Quite secluded from sensuality and unwholesome dhammas, a bhikkhu enters and
remains in the first mental theme, with thinking and pondering, and with
rejoicing and satisfaction born of seclusion.

They drench, steep, fill, and suffuse this very body with rejoicing and
satisfaction born of seclusion, so no part of the whole body remains untouched.

It is like a skilled bath attendant who sprinkles bath powder with water and
kneads it until the whole ball is saturated inside and out without dripping.

As the bhikkhu remains diligent, ardent, and committed, thoughts tied to
household life are given up. The heart settles within, grows quiet, becomes
unified, and is mentally composed.

This too is how a bhikkhu develops kāyagatā sati.

### The Second Mental Theme

With thinking and pondering stilled, with inner clarity and unification of
mind, a bhikkhu enters and remains in the second mental theme, with rejoicing
and satisfaction born of composure.

They fill and suffuse this very body with rejoicing and satisfaction born of
composure, so no part remains untouched.

It is like a deep lake fed by a cool spring from within, with no outside inlet
and no rain. The cool water fills and suffuses the whole lake.

As the bhikkhu remains diligent, ardent, and committed, thoughts tied to
household life are given up. The heart settles within, grows quiet, becomes
unified, and is mentally composed.

This too is how a bhikkhu develops kāyagatā sati.

### The Third Mental Theme

With the fading of rejoicing, a bhikkhu remains in dynamic balance,
remembering and clearly knowing, and experiences ease in the body. They enter
and remain in the third mental theme, of which the noble ones say: 'A person of
dynamic balance and remembering stays at ease.'

They fill and suffuse this very body with satisfaction free of rejoicing, so no
part remains untouched.

It is like blue, red, or white lotuses born and growing underwater. Cool water
fills and suffuses them from root to tip.

As the bhikkhu remains diligent, ardent, and committed, thoughts tied to
household life are given up. The heart settles within, grows quiet, becomes
unified, and is mentally composed.

This too is how a bhikkhu develops kāyagatā sati.

### The Fourth Mental Theme

With ease and discomfort abandoned, and with gladness and sadness already
ended, a bhikkhu enters and remains in the fourth mental theme, without
discomfort or ease, purified by dynamic balance and remembering.

They sit suffusing this very body with a pure and bright mind, so no part of the
whole body remains untouched.

It is like a person sitting wrapped from head to foot in a white cloth, with no
part of the body left uncovered.

As the bhikkhu remains diligent, ardent, and committed, thoughts tied to
household life are given up. The heart settles within, grows quiet, becomes
unified, and is mentally composed.

This too is how a bhikkhu develops kāyagatā sati.

### Everything That Supports Knowledge

For anyone who has developed and cultivated kāyagatā sati, every wholesome
quality that supports knowledge is included within it.

It is like encompassing the great ocean in the mind: every small river that
flows into the ocean is included within it. In the same way, all wholesome
qualities that support knowledge are included within developed and cultivated
kāyagatā sati.

### Open to Māra—or Not

When someone has not developed and cultivated kāyagatā sati, Māra finds an
opening and a foothold in them.

It is like throwing a heavy stone into a mound of wet clay: the stone easily
sinks in. It is like rubbing a dry fire-stick against dry wood: fire easily
appears. It is like bringing a load of water to an empty jar: there is room to
pour it in.

But when someone has developed and cultivated kāyagatā sati, Māra finds no
opening and no foothold in them.

It is like throwing a light ball of thread at a solid hardwood door: it cannot
sink in. It is like rubbing a fire-stick against wet, sappy wood: no fire
appears. It is like bringing more water to a jar already full to the brim:
there is nowhere to pour it.

### A Workable Heart

When someone has developed and cultivated kāyagatā sati, they can turn the
heart toward anything that can be directly known and realized. Whenever the
necessary basis is present, they become capable of witnessing it directly.

It is like a full water jar: a strong person can tip it in any direction and
water flows. It is like a square pond full to the brim: open the bank on any
side and water flows. It is like a well-trained chariot standing on level
ground: a skilled driver takes the reins and goes forward or back wherever
they choose.

### Ten Benefits

When kāyagatā sati has been practiced, developed, cultivated, made a vehicle
and a basis, maintained, consolidated, and properly undertaken, ten benefits
can be expected.

1. A bhikkhu can endure discontent and attraction. They overcome discontent
   when it arises instead of being overcome by it.
2. They can endure fear and dread. They overcome fear and dread when it arises
   instead of being overcome by it.
3. They can endure cold and heat, hunger and thirst, flies and mosquitoes,
   wind, sun, and creeping things. They can endure harsh and unwelcome speech
   and painful bodily feelings that are sharp, severe, unpleasant, and even
   life-threatening.
4. They can readily enter the four mental themes—higher states of mind that
   provide a satisfying way of living in this very life.
5. They can wield many kinds of psychic power: becoming one from many and many
   from one, appearing and disappearing, passing through barriers, moving
   through earth or water, walking on water, flying through the sky, touching
   the sun and moon, and exercising bodily mastery as far as the Brahmā worlds.
6. With the divine ear, purified and beyond the human, they hear human and
   divine sounds, whether far or near.
7. They encompass the minds of others with their own and know a heart with or
   without passion, aversion, or delusion; contracted or distracted; expansive
   or unexpansive; surpassable or unsurpassable; composed or uncomposed; freed
   or unfreed.
8. They recollect many past lives, from one or two births through many eons of
   world-contraction and expansion, remembering their circumstances and how
   they passed from one life to another.
9. With the divine eye, purified and beyond the human, they see beings passing
   away and reappearing, low and high, beautiful and ugly, in good and bad
   destinations, and understand how beings move on according to their actions.
10. With the outflows worn away, they realize and enter the freedom of heart
    and freedom through discernment in this very life by their own higher
    knowing.

These are the ten benefits that can be expected when kāyagatā sati has been
practiced, developed, cultivated, made a vehicle and a basis, maintained,
consolidated, and properly undertaken."

That is what the Buddha said. The bhikkhus were satisfied and delighted in his
words.

The ninth discourse, Kāyagatāsati Sutta, is finished.
