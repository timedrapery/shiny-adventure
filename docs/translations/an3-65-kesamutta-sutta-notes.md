# AN 3.65 Translation Notes

This document is the companion rationale for
[AN 3.65: Kesamutta Sutta](an3-65-kesamutta-sutta.md). The main translation
is the primary study surface; this file records debated translation choices,
source-audit calls, and edition notes that govern it.

## Source Base

- Primary source: the Pali text of AN 3.65 as segmented in SuttaCentral's
  Bilara data.
- Control surface: the repository's current term policy, especially `lobha`,
  `dosa`, `moha`, `kusala`, `akusala`, `mettā`, `karuṇā`, `muditā`,
  `upekkhā`, `citta`, `sampajañña`, `sati`, `abhijjhā`, and `byāpāda`.
- Working method: the discourse was governed as the repository's control
  surface for the ten-criterion epistemological instruction and the four
  consolations that arise for a practitioner whose feeling mind is free of
  greed, aversion, and delusion.

## Governing Decisions

- The ten-criterion list (`mā anussavena...`) is rendered with the following
  ten terms: oral transmission (`anussava`), lineage (`paramparā`), hearsay
  (`itikirā`), scriptural authority (`piṭakasampadāna`), logical
  argumentation (`takka`), inference (`naya`), thinking through the apparent
  reasons (`ākāraparivitakka`), agreeing with a view after reflecting on it
  (`diṭṭhinijjhānakkhanti`), the apparent competence of a speaker
  (`bhabbarūpatā`), and 'this recluse is our teacher' (`samaṇo no garū`).
  The instruction does not say these are wrong in themselves — it says they
  are each insufficient alone as grounds for accepting or rejecting a
  teaching. The criterion that does count: knowing for oneself whether these
  things are wholesome or unwholesome, blameworthy or blameless, criticized
  or praised by those with discernment, and whether when undertaken they lead
  to harm or to welfare.
- `lobha` is rendered `greed` (major lexicon entry). `Lobha` and `rāga` are
  related but distinct: `rāga` (passion) names the affective pull toward
  pleasure; `lobha` (greed) names the acquisitive grasping that results.
  In AN 3.65 the three roots `lobha / dosa / moha` are used in their active,
  behavioural sense — they are what overwhelms (`abhibhūto`) the person and
  takes over the feeling mind, leading to misconduct.
- `dosa` is rendered `aversion` (house default). In the brahmavihāra
  description that follows the three-roots analysis, `byāpāda` (aversive
  distraction) is used in the phrase `vigatabyāpādo` (free of aversive
  distraction) — the compound form with the same root. Both `dosa` and
  `byāpāda` are rendered consistently using the established house term family.
- `kusala` / `akusala` are rendered `wholesome` / `unwholesome` (major
  lexicon entries). The four-fold test in each triad — unwholesome, blameworthy,
  criticized by those with discernment, leads to harm — uses these as its
  first criterion. The rendering preserves the technical precision of the
  Pali pair without importing the "skillful/unskillful" register (which
  belongs to a different translation tradition).
- `viññū` is rendered `those with discernment`. `Viññū` = the wise, those
  who understand, the discerning. Since the house renders `paññā` as
  `discernment`, the adjectival noun `viññū` is rendered "those with
  discernment" — maintaining family coherence.
- `mettā` is rendered `friendliness` (major lexicon entry). `Karuṇā` is
  rendered `kindness towards others` (major). `Muditā` is rendered `gladness`
  (major). `Upekkhā` is rendered `dynamic balance` (major). These four
  brahmavihāra terms use their established house renderings.
- The brahmavihāra formula — `ekaṁ disaṁ pharitvā viharati, tathā dutiyaṁ,
  tathā tatiyaṁ, tathā catutthaṁ, iti uddhamadho tiriyaṁ sabbadhi sabbattatāya
  sabbāvantaṁ lokaṁ ... cetasā vipulena mahaggatena appamāṇena averena
  abyāpajjhena pharitvā viharati` — is rendered: "dwells pervading one
  direction with a feeling mind filled with [X], likewise the second, likewise
  the third, likewise the fourth — thus above, below, across, everywhere, for
  all as for oneself, they dwell pervading the entire world with a feeling mind
  filled with [X], vast, expansive, boundless, without enmity and without ill
  will." The phrase `sabbattatāya` (for all as for oneself) is translated
  literally rather than collapsed into "everywhere."
- `assāsa` is rendered `consolation` (no standalone lexicon entry). `Assāsa`
  literally = in-breath, and metaphorically = relief, reassurance, consolation.
  `Cattāro assāsā` = four consolations. The four consolations are explicitly
  not arguments for or against the afterlife — they function as practical
  reassurances that are won regardless of which metaphysical view turns out to
  be correct. This is the sutta's key contribution to the epistemological
  theme: even without resolving the afterlife question, the practitioner is
  not at risk. The consolations are won in this very life (`diṭṭheva dhamme`).
- `sace... sace... sace... sace...` (if... if... if... if...) — the
  four consolations each begin with `sace` (if). This is not hedging; it is
  the explicit structure of the argument: the consolations hold under all four
  logical possibilities regarding karma and the afterlife. No single position
  needs to be established for the practice to be worthwhile.
- `averacitto abyāpajjhacitto asaṅkiliṭṭhacitto visuddhacitto` = "with a
  feeling mind free of enmity, free of ill will, uncorrupted and pure." These
  four qualities describe the noble disciple after having cultivated the four
  brahmavihāras. They repeat the same four-quality description twice in the
  discourse — once in the Buddha's statement (an3.65:43, an3.65:47)
  and once in the Kālāmas' confirmation (an3.65:48, an3.65:52). The
  repetition is preserved.
- The sutta title in the Bilara is `Kesamuttisutta` (named for Kesamutta, the
  Kālāma town). The popular name "Kālāma Sutta" is noted in the Editorial
  Note.

  readability.

  abbreviated in the Bilara — the middle two consolations are collapsed to
  empty strings. These are expanded in full in the translation.

## Editorial Presentation

- The main translation is divided into seven sections: `The Setting`,
  `The Kālāmas' Uncertainty`, `The Ten Criteria`, `The Three Roots of Harm`,
  `The Three Roots of Welfare`, `Friendliness, Kindness, Gladness, and Dynamic
  Balance`, `The Four Consolations`, and `Going for Refuge`.
- The ten-criterion list appears three times in the sutta: in the initial
  instruction (an3.65:4.1), in the restatement after the negative triad
  (an3.65:25.3), and in the restatement after the positive triad
  (an3.65:41.3). All three are given in full, consistent with the policy of
  preserving repetition.
- The four consolations are given twice — once by the Buddha and once
  in confirmation by the Kālāmas. Both statements are preserved in full.
