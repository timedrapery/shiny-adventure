# MN 63: Cūḷamālukya Sutta

## Editorial Note

- This is a repo-native study translation of MN 63, designed to be readable as
  a single Markdown document.
- Companion translator notes:
  [MN 63 Translation Notes](mn63-culamalukya-sutta-notes.md)
- Source basis: the Pali text of MN 63 as segmented in SuttaCentral's Bilara
  data. The sutta is titled `Cūḷamālukyasutta` (the shorter discourse to
  Mālukyaputta), paired with MN 64 `Mahāmālukyasutta` (the longer discourse to
  the same disciple).
- House choices that most affect this sutta: `brahmacariya` is rendered
  `spiritual life`; `loka` is rendered `world`; `tathāgata` remains
  untranslated as `Tathāgata`; `nibbidā` is rendered `disenchantment`;
  `virāga` is rendered `fading`; `nirodha` is rendered `cessation`; `upasama`
  is rendered `calming`; `abhiññā` is rendered `higher knowing`; `sambodha` is
  rendered `awakening`; `nibbāna` remains untranslated; `dukkha` is rendered
  `dissatisfaction`.
- This edition treats MN 63 as the repository's control surface for the
  undeclared (`avyākata`) doctrinal family, for the ten undeclared points
  themselves, and for the poisoned-arrow simile against demanding metaphysical
  answers before practicing.

## Translation

### The Setting

So I have heard. At one time the Buddha was staying near Sāvatthī, in
Jeta's Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery.

### Mālukyaputta's Reflection

Then, while Venerable Mālukyaputta was alone in seclusion, this reflection
arose in his mind: "These points have been left undeclared by the Buddha. He
has set them aside and rejected them: 'the world is eternal,' 'the world is not
eternal,' 'the world is finite,' 'the world is infinite,' 'the soul and the
body are the same,' 'the soul is one thing and the body another,' 'the
Tathāgata exists after death,' 'the Tathāgata does not exist after death,'
'the Tathāgata both exists and does not exist after death,' 'the Tathāgata
neither exists nor does not exist after death.' The Buddha does not declare
these to me. I do not approve of this, and I do not accept it. I will go to
the Buddha and ask him about this. If the Buddha declares to me that the world
is eternal, or that the world is not
eternal, or that the world is finite, or that the world is infinite, or that
the soul and the body are the same, or that the soul is one thing and the body
another, or that the Tathāgata exists after death, or that the Tathāgata does
not exist after death, or that the Tathāgata both exists and does not exist
after death, or that the Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist after
death — then I will continue to live the spiritual life under the Buddha.
But if the Buddha does not declare these to me, then I will renounce the
training and return to the lower life of a layperson."

### Mālukyaputta's Challenge

Then, when it was evening, Venerable Mālukyaputta rose from seclusion and went
to the Buddha. He bowed to the Buddha and sat down to one side.
Seated to one side, Venerable Mālukyaputta said to the Buddha:

"Bhante, while I was alone in seclusion, this reflection arose in my mind:
'These points have been left undeclared by the Buddha. He has set them aside
and rejected them: that the world is eternal, or not eternal; that the world is
finite, or infinite; that the soul and the body are the same, or that the
soul is one thing and the body another; that the Tathāgata exists after
death, does not exist after death, both exists and does not exist after
death, or neither exists nor does not exist after death. The Buddha does not
declare these to me. I do not approve of this, and I do not accept it.'

If the Buddha knows that the world is eternal, let the Buddha
declare to me that the world is eternal. If the Buddha knows that the
world is not eternal, let the Buddha declare to me that the world is not
eternal. If the Buddha does not know whether the world is eternal or not
eternal, then, not knowing, not seeing, the honest thing for the Buddha
to say is: 'I do not know, I do not see.'

[And so with each of the remaining points: whether the world is finite or
infinite; whether the soul and the body are the same or different; and
whether the Tathāgata exists after death, does not exist after death, both
exists and does not exist after death, or neither exists nor does not exist
after death — if the Buddha knows, let him declare it; if he does not
know, let him say so.]"

### Not Promised, Not Agreed

"What do you think, Mālukyaputta — did I ever say to you, 'Come, Mālukyaputta,
live the spiritual life under me, and I will declare to you that the world is
eternal, or that the world is not eternal, or that the world is finite, or
infinite, or that the soul and the body are the same or different, or that the
Tathāgata exists after death, does not exist after death, both exists and does
not exist after death, or neither exists nor does not exist after death'?"

"No, Bhante."

"Or did you say to me, 'Bhante, I will live the spiritual life under the
Buddha, and the Buddha will declare to me that the world is
eternal...' and so on through all ten points?"

"No, Bhante."

"That being so, foolish man, who are you, and what promise are you accusing of
being broken?

Mālukyaputta, if anyone were to say, 'I will not live the spiritual life under
the Buddha until the Buddha declares to me that the world is
eternal' — that would still be undeclared by the Tathāgata, and meanwhile that
person would die.

### The Poisoned Arrow

Suppose, Mālukyaputta, a man were wounded by an arrow thickly smeared with
poison, and his friends and companions, his relatives and family, brought a
surgeon to treat him. But the man would say: 'I will not let this arrow be
pulled out until I know the name and clan of the man who wounded me; until I
know whether the man who wounded me was tall, short, or of middle height;
until I know whether the man who wounded me was dark-skinned, brown-skinned,
or golden-skinned; until I know which village, town, or city the man who
wounded me comes from; until I know whether the bow that wounded me was a
long bow or a crossbow; until I know whether the bowstring was made of fiber,
reed, sinew, hemp, or bark; until I know whether the shaft was from a wild
plant or a cultivated one; until I know what kind of feathers were bound to
the shaft — vulture, heron, hawk, peacock, or some other bird; until I know
what kind of sinew was used to wrap the shaft — ox, buffalo, deer, or monkey
sinew; until I know what sort of arrow it was — an ordinary arrow, a curved
one, a barbed one, one shaped like a calf's tooth, or one shaped like an
oleander thorn.'

That man would die, Mālukyaputta, before he ever knew these things.

In the same way, if anyone were to say, 'I will not live the spiritual life
under the Buddha until the Buddha declares to me that the world is
eternal, or that the world is not eternal, or that the world is finite, or
infinite, or that the soul and the body are the same or different, or that the
Tathāgata exists after death, does not exist after death, both exists and does
not exist after death, or neither exists nor does not exist after death' —
that would still be undeclared by the Tathāgata, and meanwhile that person
would die.

### Not Dependent On These Views

Mālukyaputta, the spiritual life does not depend on the view that the world
is eternal. Nor does the spiritual life depend on the view that the world is
not eternal. Whether the view is held that the world is eternal, or that the
world is not eternal, there is still birth, ageing and death, sorrow,
lamentation, pain, distress, and despair — and it is the ending of these,
here and now, that I declare.

The spiritual life does not depend on the view that the world is finite, nor
on the view that the world is infinite. Whether the view is held that the
world is finite, or that the world is infinite, there is still birth, ageing
and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, and despair — and it is the
ending of these, here and now, that I declare.

The spiritual life does not depend on the view that the soul and the body are
the same, nor on the view that the soul is one thing and the body another.
Whether the view is held that the soul and the body are the same, or that the
soul is one thing and the body another, there is still birth, ageing and
death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, and despair — and it is the
ending of these, here and now, that I declare.

The spiritual life does not depend on the view that the Tathāgata exists
after death, nor on the view that the Tathāgata does not exist after death,
nor on the view that the Tathāgata both exists and does not exist after
death, nor on the view that the Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist
after death. Whether any of these four views is held, there is still birth,
ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, and despair — and it is
the ending of these, here and now, that I declare.

### Undeclared And Declared

So, Mālukyaputta, understand as undeclared what I have left undeclared, and
understand as declared what I have declared.

And what have I left undeclared? 'The world is eternal' — this I have left
undeclared. 'The world is not eternal' — this I have left undeclared. 'The
world is finite' — this I have left undeclared. 'The world is infinite' —
this I have left undeclared. 'The soul and the body are the same' — this I
have left undeclared. 'The soul is one thing and the body another' — this I
have left undeclared. 'The Tathāgata exists after death' — this I have left
undeclared. 'The Tathāgata does not exist after death' — this I have left
undeclared. 'The Tathāgata both exists and does not exist after death' — this
I have left undeclared. 'The Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist
after death' — this I have left undeclared.

Why have I left this undeclared? Because it is not beneficial, it is not
relevant to the fundamentals of the spiritual life, and it does not lead to
disenchantment, fading, cessation, calming, higher knowing, awakening, or
nibbāna. That is why I have left it undeclared.

And what have I declared? 'This is dissatisfaction' — this I have declared.
'This is the origin of dissatisfaction' — this I have declared. 'This is the
cessation of dissatisfaction' — this I have declared. 'This is the way
leading to the cessation of dissatisfaction' — this I have declared.

Why have I declared this? Because it is beneficial, it is relevant to the
fundamentals of the spiritual life, and it leads to disenchantment, fading,
cessation, calming, higher knowing, awakening, and nibbāna. That is why I have
declared it.

So, Mālukyaputta, understand as undeclared what I have left undeclared, and
understand as declared what I have declared."

That is what the Buddha said. Venerable Mālukyaputta was satisfied and
welcomed what the Buddha had said.

The Cūḷamālukya Sutta, the third discourse, is finished.
